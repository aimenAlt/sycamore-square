03 · THE EVENT COMPLEX

Two halls that work apart or together

Built for weddings where men and women celebrate separately, while staying fully marketable for any event.

  • 2 × 11,000 SF

    Main rooms, 132 × 83 ft each, set between column lines with 2 interior columns each

  • 4 × 700 SF

    Bride and groom suites per hall, each with a private bath and hair, makeup and nail prep

  • 2 kitchens

    About 1,950 SF each, with rear receiving, a direct serving wall into their own hall and a controlled door between them

  • 2,400 SF

    Reservable dining room, plus a guest gallery linking the two foyers

One wedding, separated

Ladies in Hall A, men in Hall B

One family books both halls. Each side has its own screened entrance, foyer, toilets and family suites, so neither group passes through the other.

  • Mothers reach the private child and family zone directly from Hall A, without crossing the men's hall or the public play lobby.
  • One caterer can run both kitchens through the controlled connecting door.
  • Female-only service teams can be assigned to Hall A.

Two independent events

Two unrelated bookings at once

Each hall works as a complete venue: its own entrance, foyer, toilets, suites, kitchen and staff.

  • Food reaches each hall from its own kitchen, never through the other event.
  • The dining room goes to one event, or to both in separate time slots.
  • Deliveries and waste stay on the rear service drive, away from guests.

One coordinated event

One large linked event

The guest gallery opens between the two foyers under one management plan. Both kitchens serve together and the dining room becomes a shared space.

  • Both entrances and every required exit stay in use.
  • It is a linked complex, not one unobstructed ballroom.

The 18,000 SF play and gaming center has its own public entrance and also serves wedding families through a supervised private zone. Guest capacity, fixture counts and childcare licensing are still to be confirmed.

04 · FUEL AND CONVENIENCE OPTIONS

Three ways to handle fuel

The frontage on Sycamore View is the preferred spot. The Summer Avenue pad fits a station more easily, and dropping fuel removes the most risk. All three were tested with the same module sizes.

Frontage · preferred

Pumps on the road, store beside them

Canopy:
72 × 40 ft, 3 islands, 6 fueling positions, plus a 40 × 20 ft tank reservation. It sits about 91 ft from Sycamore View, fed straight off the existing driveway.
Store:
4,000 SF (80 × 50 ft), 41 ft from the canopy and facing it.
Why split:
The frontage has only about 69 ft of its own road edge. A store and pumps in one block would sit 200–240 ft back from the road.
Watch:
queues at the shared driveway during events, tanker turning paths, and the former 1981 quick-lube pad (Phase I check).

5800 Summer · fallback

The whole station on one pad

Fit:
the 4,000 SF store, 6-position canopy and aisles fit as one block on the 5800 Summer pad, about 87 ft from Summer Ave.
Road:
Summer Ave / US 70, 21,290 vehicles per day, with about 240 ft of frontage across both pads.
Access:
Uses the existing Summer Ave drive, which stays clear for venue traffic.
Trade-off:
it uses pad and south event parking, and takes the best pad away from other income. The floodplain edge lies just to the west.

No fuel

Store inside the building, no pumps

The 4,000 SF convenience store moves into the retail program, taken from its 35,000 SF rather than added to it. No new building goes up on the frontage.

  • No fuel tanks, fuel permits or fuel environmental exposure
  • Least capital and fewest approvals of the three options
  • The frontage stays free for signage, landscaping or another pad use
  • Gives up fuel income and the road-front convenience draw

05 · LEGAL & REGULATORY

Two issues decide the plan

They are flood exposure, and whether CMU-3 zoning allows event halls, fuel sales and supervised childcare. Both can be answered early and cheaply.

FLOOD · THE BIGGEST COST QUESTION

About 74% of the building and about 4.0 of the 11.5 acres in the three original parcels lie in the 100-year floodplain.

Under federal flood rules (44 CFR 59.1), a project costing 50% or more of the structure's market value is a substantial improvement. The whole building must then meet current flood standards. A full event conversion will very likely cross that line.

The first test is a surveyor's elevation certificate. If the building sits above the flood elevation, FEMA can remove it from the zone with a Letter of Map Amendment.

Confirmed from records

  • CMU-3 zoning on all three parcels; residential and agricultural zoning behind the building
  • FEMA Zone AE, panel 47157C0311G; flood insurance needed for federally backed loans
  • 11.88 ac across the four recorded parcels, against 11.72 ac stated by the broker
  • The frontage is a separate 0.38 ac record that must be named in the contract
  • A 1981 quick-lube once stood on the frontage; it needs a Phase I environmental check

Found, not yet verified

  • Separate parcel numbers alone do not prove the pads can be sold separately
  • Shared access and parking agreements likely transfer with title
  • Fuel tanks fall under the Tennessee UST program, which requires a 15-day pre-installation notice
  • Summer Ave is US-70; driveway changes likely need a state permit
  • Converting an office to an assembly use triggers current fire, exit, restroom and ADA rules

Open questions

  • Are event halls, fuel sales and childcare permitted in CMU-3? A zoning verification letter settles it.
  • Does supervised care of guests' children need a state child care license?
  • Can public play hours be limited by sex or family status?
  • What parking minimums apply, and how many stalls really exist?

06 · DUE DILIGENCE

What closes the gaps

The purchase contract should carry a due-diligence period covering each item, with the right to exit. Flood and zoning come first because they decide whether the plan works.

  1. 01ALTA boundary and topographic survey, reconciling the acreage and the frontage line
  2. 02Title commitment, plats, easements and any access or parking agreements
  3. 03FEMA elevation certificate and effective flood map, plus a LOMA if the building sits above flood elevation
  4. 04Meeting with the Memphis floodplain administrator on how substantial improvement is measured
  5. 05Zoning verification letter for every planned use and parcel
  6. 06Phase I Environmental Site Assessment, covering the former quick-lube
  7. 07Roof, structural and MEP assessment, including measured columns, clear heights and the sprinkler system
  8. 08Fuel operator concept and term sheet; contractor pricing in place of placeholder cost ranges
