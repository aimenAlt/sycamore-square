Public version · 18 Oct 2026

The whole project, run by the owners, buying included

Run by the owners, the whole project earns its money back in 14.2 to 16.6 years in the realistic case. That counts everything: buying the property at an assumed price of $3.0M with fees and closing, the base building work, the hall fit-out, design, leasing and holding costs. A typical investment property earns back in about 12 years, and the goal is 10 years or less; only the best case gets there.

The fit-out covers hall finishes, lighting and sound at about $80 / $55 / $36 per SF, and the two kitchens (hoods, grease trap, cold storage) at about $338 / $235 / $164 per SF, at Memphis prices with the ownership group hiring each trade directly. The market supports the product:

  • Size. Each 11,000 SF room seats about 650–700 at tables with a dance floor, stage and buffet; together the halls seat about 1,300–1,450. A movable wall splits each room for two 150–350 guest events, so the venue can run up to four events at once, one very large wedding across both rooms, or one coordinated event. The rooms are column-free once the 4 existing columns are removed.
  • Saturday prices. Comparable Memphis halls ask about $7,000–9,000 for a 300–400 guest Saturday. The Palace at Cordova asks $7,000 on Saturday, $6,500 on Friday, $6,000 on Sunday and $5,500 Monday–Thursday for 11 hours, and sells dates inside 30 days from $3,500. The Columns ballroom is $8,550 for 10 am to midnight. Premium venues ask more: the Cadre Building $12,200 and Memphis Botanic Garden $10,000. A full room for 650–700 guests rents for $11,000–15,000 at the few venues that size, such as The Kent.
  • Catering prices. Memphis buffets run $18–35 per guest and plated meals $28–52, plus a 20–24% service charge: Esplanade Memphis charges 20% and The Peabody 23%. Marriott Memphis East lists $34–52 per person.
  • Profit levels. Catering businesses sold in 2021–25 kept a median of about 23% of sales as profit (BizBuySell). The halls' realistic case keeps 27% before building costs, because room rental, which costs little to deliver, is about 45% of sales.
  • Who books. Weddings of every background, plus quinceañeras, corporate, church, community and celebration events. Large Arab, South Asian, African and Hispanic weddings and celebrations are among the customer bases; about 1,100–1,200 girls in the metro's Hispanic community turn 15 each year (Census). The largest banquet halls listed in Jackson, Mississippi hold about 350 guests (Zola, Jackson MS), so very large events may also come from there.
  • Bookings. 220 events a year is a year-3 number for a mixed business, not a wedding-only one. Mixed-use venues run 100–200+ events a year and wedding-only venues 30–60 (MMCG); one industry estimate puts the average venue at 217 (ProjectionHub). Two rooms have only 104 Saturdays a year, so at most about 40% of 220 events can fall on a Saturday. Couples book their venue 12–18 months ahead, so a fair estimate is about 110–140 events in year 1 and 170–200 in year 2. The average US wedding has about 117 guests (The Knot), which is why each room needs its movable wall: without it, bookings could stay near 120–160 a year.
  • Competition. No hall outside the hotels seating 500 or more was found in northeast Memphis, Raleigh or Bartlett. The nearest are in Cordova and Germantown (FedEx Event Center, about 500 seated; Esplanade, 400) and downtown (The Kent, 600) (Memphis Magazine). The 900-seat ballroom at the former Memphis Vitality Hotel has closed.
  • Capacity cap. Post capacities for seating at tables, about 1,470 for both halls together. Chairs-only ceremonies for 3,000 or more would push the building into a stricter earthquake-safety class (see the Landlord View risks).

Fuel station, built by a fuel company on a ground lease

A fuel company would build and run the station and pay the owners ground rent. At realistic volumes it keeps only about $166k a year after a $95k ground rent, on a $3.8M build: about $4 a year for every $100 it spends. So a deal is not assured, and the owners should not build or run the station themselves. In the worst case no fuel company signs, so the plans count no ground rent.

To keep $10 a year for every $100 it spends, a fuel company would need about 150,000 gallons a month at realistic margins, about the national average. National 2024 averages were 150,157 gallons a month, a 43.5¢ margin per gallon (35.2¢ after card fees) and $248,201 of store sales a month (NACS State of the Industry). In 2025 margins averaged over 40¢ (NACS Magazine). Competition is heavy: thirteen stations sit within about 1.4 miles of the I-40 Sycamore View exit (iExit).

Ground rent is about $80k–$130k a year from a regional operator and $150k–$200k from a national brand; that range is a planning judgment. Murphy USA in Murfreesboro pays $200k a year. The lease must make the fuel company responsible for its tanks, spills and cleanup. Tennessee charges $125 a year per tank compartment; the state suspended those yearly fees for five years from 1 July 2021, so check their status now (TDEC). Gasoline tax is 27.4¢ a gallon (MTAS).

A typical 1,500 SF shop tenant

A 1,500 SF shop needs about $252k of sales a year to afford realistic rent. That suits service shops, not fashion. A common rule of thumb keeps rent plus building costs near 10% of a shop's sales; restaurants nationally spend about 5–6% (National Restaurant Association).

To check before committing: a local leasing broker's view of rents and timing.

Flex space, leased to trade and storage users

About 25,559 SF, with its own west entrance, is held as flex space for contractors, showrooms, light assembly and storage users, who mostly need clear height, a roll-up door and parking. The full plan splits it into rentable units; the efficient plan leaves it open until it is leased.

Rent against the market. Flex space averages $7.49 per SF in Memphis and $10.50 in the Northeast area (Cushman & Wakefield Q3 2026). The study's $7.50 is the citywide average, below this part of town, which leaves room for the deep-site location.

Why there is no tenant sales test here. A shop pays its rent out of sales made on site, so its rent can be checked against the sales it needs. A flex tenant mostly earns its money elsewhere: a contractor on job sites, a storage user from its own customers. Its rent is a cost of space, so the test is the market rent above and how fast the space leases. The worst case leases only half of it.

Food pad on Summer Ave, a restaurant on a ground lease

The 5800 Summer pad (1.353 ac) beside Summer Avenue could take a restaurant that builds its own building and pays the owners ground rent. The study counts it only in the best case, at $70k a year, for three reasons: the study has no restaurant lined up; the same pad is the fallback site for the fuel station (layout F2); and TDOT is widening Summer Avenue past it until June 2028 (TDOT), which affects its driveways.

What it would add. In the lease-everything plan, adding the pad in the realistic case adds about $62k a year to profit and shortens the earn-back from 23.6 to 21.6 years (Landlord View, ways to improve it). In the owner-run plans it adds the same rent to a larger profit, so the effect on their earn-back is smaller.

Why it cannot go further at this stage. The study has no build cost or sales data for a restaurant on this pad, and a restaurant's own numbers depend on the brand. Until a restaurant or a restaurant broker shows interest, it stays a best-case upside. To move it forward: ask a local restaurant broker, ask the City whether 5800 Summer can be leased or sold on its own, and ask TDOT how the widening affects its driveways. The Engineering and City package carries both questions.
